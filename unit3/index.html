Chapter 7

Physical development, health, motor skills
Growth in early childhood
Growth is slower than in infancy, and body proportions gradually become more adultlike.
The torso lengthens, and the head becomes smaller in proportion to the rest of the body.
Tap card to reveal
A preschooler becomes taller and leaner instead of keeping a toddler's rounder body proportions.
Healthy eating habits
Offer balanced meals and recognize that appetite can vary without turning food into a power struggle.
Keep mealtimes pleasant, limit sugary snacks, and avoid bribing children with dessert.
Tap card to reveal
A caregiver offers a nutritious lunch and lets a child respond to hunger and fullness rather than insisting on a clean plate.
Prefrontal cortex
The brain area behind the forehead that supports planning, emotional regulation, and control of behavior.
Maturation and practice help children think ahead and manage impulses.
Tap card to reveal
A child pauses before grabbing a game piece and thinks about whose turn comes next.
Corpus callosum
The bundle of nerve fibers connecting the brain's left and right hemispheres.
Growth during early childhood supports coordination between the two sides.
Tap card to reveal
A child increasingly coordinates information and movements that involve both brain hemispheres.
Gross motor skills
Movements that mainly use large muscle groups and involve the whole body.
Running, hopping, jumping, and balancing provide practice.
Tap card to reveal
Children hop between chalk circles and climb over low obstacles on a playground.
Fine motor skills
Small, controlled movements of the hands and fingers that support drawing, dressing, and manipulating objects.
Practice with play dough, beads, drawing tools, and child-safe scissors builds dexterity.
Tap card to reveal
A child rolls small pieces of dough and cuts paper along a line.
Sleep terrors and nightmares
Sleep terrors involve sudden intense fear during deep NREM sleep, usually early in the night.
Nightmares are frightening dreams associated with REM sleep, often later in the night.
Tap card to reveal
A sleeping child screams early in the night, appears frightened, and is difficult to awaken during a sleep terror.
Toilet training readiness
Physical and emotional readiness matter more than reaching a particular birthday.
Signs include staying dry for about two hours, communicating a need to go, and following simple directions.
Tap card to reveal
A child notices a wet diaper, asks to use the toilet, and can pull clothing down with little help.
Enuresis and encopresis
The chapter uses enuresis for repeated urination into bed or clothing after age five; encopresis refers to repeated passage of feces in inappropriate places.
These terms describe different elimination concerns; an isolated accident does not establish a diagnosis.
Tap card to reveal
When reviewing the terms, connect enuresis with urine and encopresis with feces.
Body curiosity and privacy
Curiosity about bodies is common in early childhood. Adults can respond calmly while teaching privacy and appropriate boundaries.
Give clear answers without frightening or shaming children.
Tap card to reveal
A caregiver answers a question about body parts using simple language and explains which activities belong in private.
Oral health
Regular brushing with fluoride toothpaste and dental care help prevent tooth decay.
Primary teeth matter for eating, speaking, and daily comfort.
Tap card to reveal
A caregiver supervises a preschooler's toothbrushing instead of waiting for permanent teeth to appear.
Food allergies
A food allergy involves an immune response to a particular food, rather than simply disliking its taste or texture.
Caregivers need to know a child's allergies and be prepared to follow the child's emergency plan.
Tap card to reveal
A teacher checks a child's documented allergy information before offering a classroom snack.
Poisoning prevention
Medicines and toxic household products should be locked away in their original containers, out of children's sight and reach.
Original packaging helps prevent confusion; a warning label alone cannot protect a preschooler.
Tap card to reveal
A caregiver stores cleaning products in a locked cabinet instead of pouring them into a drink bottle.

Chapter 8

Cognitive development, learning, language
Preoperational thought and symbols
In Piaget's preoperational stage, children increasingly use words, images, and objects to represent other things.
Symbolic thinking supports pretend play, even while logical operations are still developing.
Tap card to reveal
A child pretends that a small cardboard tube is a telescope.
Egocentrism
Difficulty recognizing that another person may see or understand a situation differently.
In Piaget's theory, this describes a limitation in perspective taking rather than deliberate selfishness.
Tap card to reveal
A child looking at one side of a model assumes a person on the opposite side sees the same view.
Animism
Attributing life, feelings, or intentions to objects that are not alive.
Tap card to reveal
A child says a toy truck is angry because it has been left outside.
Centration
Focusing on one characteristic of a situation while overlooking other relevant features.
Centration can help explain errors on conservation tasks.
Tap card to reveal
A child compares two glasses only by their height and ignores their different widths.
Conservation
Understanding that changing an object's appearance or arrangement does not necessarily change its quantity.
Pouring, spreading out, or dividing something can change its appearance without adding or removing any.
Tap card to reveal
A child recognizes that a row of counters still has the same number after the counters are spread farther apart.
Zone of proximal development
The range of tasks a child can complete with guidance but cannot yet complete independently.
Vygotsky emphasized learning through interaction with more knowledgeable adults and peers.
Tap card to reveal
A child can solve a new puzzle with a few hints but cannot finish it alone yet.
Scaffolding
Temporary support that helps a child complete a task and is gradually reduced as competence increases.
The support changes in response to what the child needs.
Tap card to reveal
A teacher first demonstrates a task, then gives hints, and eventually lets the child work independently.
Private speech
Speech directed to oneself that can help guide thinking and behavior.
Vygotsky viewed talking through a task as a useful step toward internal speech.
Tap card to reveal
While building a tower, a child says, "Big block first. Now a smaller one."
Executive function
Self-regulatory processes that help a person control impulses, shift strategies, and work toward goals.
Inhibitory control and cognitive flexibility continue developing across childhood.
Tap card to reveal
A child stops following an old sorting rule and switches to a new rule when the teacher changes the game.
Working memory
The system used to hold and actively work with information during current conscious activity.
Young children have a more limited working memory capacity than older children and adults.
Tap card to reveal
A child remembers a short set of directions while collecting the materials needed for an activity.
Fast-mapping
Rapidly connecting a new word with a concept, often after very limited exposure.
A quick initial connection supports vocabulary growth; understanding can deepen with later experience.
Tap card to reveal
After hearing a teacher name an unfamiliar tool, a child connects the new word with that object.
Overregularization
Applying a grammar rule to a word that is an exception to the rule.
These errors can show that a child has learned a rule and is trying to use it.
Tap card to reveal
After learning to add -ed for the past tense, a child says "runned" instead of "ran."
Theory of mind and false belief
Theory of mind involves understanding other people's mental states, including that their beliefs may differ from reality.
A false-belief task requires separating what you know from what another person believes.
Tap card to reveal
A child sees a toy moved while its owner is away and predicts the owner will first search in the original hiding place.
Individualized support for autism
Children with autism spectrum disorder have differing strengths and support needs. Early support should be tailored to the individual.
Support may address communication, learning, daily living, and participation; there is no single approach that suits everyone.
Tap card to reveal
A team chooses communication supports and classroom strategies based on a particular child's needs and strengths.

Chapter 9

Social-emotional development, relationships, play
Self-concept and self-esteem
Self-concept is a description of who you are; self-esteem is an evaluation of your worth or qualities.
Preschool self-descriptions often emphasize appearance, preferred activities, and possessions.
Tap card to reveal
"I have brown hair and I like drawing" describes self-concept; "I feel good about myself" expresses self-esteem.
Delayed gratification
Passing up a smaller immediate reward in order to wait for a larger reward.
This is one aspect of developing self-control.
Tap card to reveal
A child chooses to wait for two stickers later rather than take one sticker right away.
Erikson's initiative versus guilt
Preschoolers develop initiative when they have opportunities to plan activities, make choices, and act on ideas.
Encouragement of effort supports initiative; excessive criticism and control can foster guilt.
Tap card to reveal
An adult helps a child plan a pretend market and accepts small mistakes as part of learning.
Gender role socialization
Learning cultural expectations about gender through messages, reinforcement, and observation.
Parents, peers, teachers, and media can all contribute to these expectations.
Tap card to reveal
A child notices that certain toys are repeatedly described as being "for boys" or "for girls" and learns a cultural expectation.
Authoritative parenting
A style combining warmth and responsiveness with clear, reasonable expectations and consistent limits.
Parents explain rules and allow discussion while maintaining guidance.
Tap card to reveal
A parent acknowledges a child's disappointment, explains the bedtime rule, and follows through calmly.
Authoritarian parenting
A style emphasizing obedience and strict control, with little warmth or room for discussion.
Do not confuse authoritarian with authoritative: both can set limits, but their responsiveness differs.
Tap card to reveal
A parent demands that a child obey immediately and refuses to discuss the reason for a rule.
Permissive parenting
A style involving warmth and responsiveness but few demands, limits, or consistent expectations.
Uninvolved parenting differs by also showing low responsiveness and engagement.
Tap card to reveal
A parent is affectionate but lets a preschooler decide nearly all routines and rules.
High-quality child care
Responsive relationships, manageable group sizes, and appropriate teacher-child ratios support care quality.
Consider the everyday interactions and environment, not just the program's name or cost.
Tap card to reveal
Teachers notice children's cues, respond supportively, and can give individual attention within a manageable group.
Parallel play
Children play alongside one another with similar materials without directly coordinating their activities.
Playing nearby is different from sharing a common goal.
Tap card to reveal
Two children sit beside one another with toy animals but create separate games without interacting.
Associative play
Children interact and share materials during play without organizing around one common goal.
Distinguish it from parallel play, where direct interaction is limited.
Tap card to reveal
Children talk and exchange art supplies while each makes a separate picture.
Cooperative play
Children coordinate their play to achieve a shared goal, often taking different roles.
The common plan distinguishes cooperative from associative play.
Tap card to reveal
Children assign construction jobs and work together to build one pretend zoo.
Conscience
Internal standards of conduct that help guide a child's judgments and behavior.
Conscience develops through relationships, self-regulation, and understanding of moral expectations.
Tap card to reveal
A child decides to return a borrowed toy because keeping it would feel wrong, even when no adult is watching.
Stress and caring relationships
Prolonged severe stress can disrupt development, while caring, dependable adults can help buffer its effects.
Ordinary everyday challenges differ from prolonged severe stress without adequate support.
Tap card to reveal
A child facing a difficult family situation receives steady comfort and predictable routines from a trusted adult.
